What should a contractor's work include?

A contractor's role depends on the project and the agreement. For a larger remodel, that role may include organizing the work, coordinating trades, scheduling, sourcing materials, and keeping the homeowner informed. For a smaller job, the scope may be limited to specific tasks.

Before work starts, the written scope should name the rooms or areas involved, the work to be done, and the materials or selections included. It should also explain what is excluded, who handles permits if they are required, how changes are approved, and how you will receive updates. Don't rely on a general phrase like “complete bathroom remodel” to explain every detail.

What does a local professional check before quoting?

A useful first visit is more than a quick look at the room. The contractor should ask what you want to change, how you use the space, what must stay, and whether you have plans, product selections, or a target time frame. Those answers help define the work before anyone treats an early estimate as final.

  • Visible conditions, access to the work area, and any known history of leaks, repairs, or previous alterations.
  • Whether the project may involve plumbing, electrical, structural, or other trade work that needs coordination.
  • Which decisions are settled and which are still open, such as tile, fixtures, cabinets, or finishes.

Ask the contractor to separate confirmed work from items that cannot be assessed until a wall, floor, or ceiling is opened. That distinction can make a proposal easier to compare and reduce surprise when hidden conditions come up.

What changes the cost of a contractor project?

The price depends on the work described, not just the room name or square footage. Demolition, the condition of what is behind finished surfaces, material choices, custom details, and the number of trades can all change the amount of labor and coordination involved.

A proposal may also be affected by access, disposal needs, permit or inspection requirements, and whether the homeowner is supplying any materials. Ask which selections are included, whether allowances are listed, and what is treated as extra work. No estimate can account for every concealed condition before it is visible, so ask how added work will be documented and approved.

For a fair comparison, line up proposals by scope and assumptions rather than choosing the lowest total on its own. A lower number may leave out a task, a material, or coordination that another proposal includes.

What can affect the schedule?

Timing depends on the scope, the order of the work, material availability, trade schedules, and any required permits or inspections. A contractor should explain the expected sequence and identify decisions or deliveries that need to happen before a crew can move forward.

Ask what the schedule assumes and what could change it. If opening a wall may reveal damage or outdated work, the contractor can explain how that discovery would be handled, but should not promise a firm finish date without accounting for unknown conditions.

What questions should you ask before you hire?

  • Do you serve my address, and have you handled this kind of project?
  • What exactly is included in the written scope, and what is excluded?
  • Who will be my day-to-day contact, and who coordinates any other trades?
  • How are material selections, allowances, and homeowner-supplied items recorded?
  • Who checks whether permits or inspections are required for this work?
  • How do you document and price a change or an unexpected condition?
  • What schedule are you proposing, and what could affect it?
  • What payment schedule and written agreement will we use?

Listen for clear answers that match the proposal. A contractor who explains assumptions and limits plainly gives you a better basis for deciding than one who offers a quick promise without defining the work.

Before you hire checklist

  • Confirm the contractor serves your property address and handles your type of project.
  • Review the written scope, included materials, exclusions, and change process.
  • Clarify who will coordinate trades, permits, inspections, and day-to-day updates.
  • Compare proposals using the same work details and schedule assumptions.
  • Make sure the payment terms and agreement are in writing before work begins.

What hiring mistakes should you avoid?

  • Choosing from a search result without confirming that the provider covers your address.
  • Comparing totals that describe different materials, tasks, or exclusions.
  • Starting work with verbal promises instead of a written scope and change process.
  • Assuming a contractor will handle permits, selections, cleanup, or trade coordination without seeing it in the agreement.
  • Making a major selection late without asking whether it affects ordering or the work sequence.

Pause if you don't understand a term in the proposal or can't tell who is responsible for a task. Ask for a plain-language explanation and a written revision before signing.
