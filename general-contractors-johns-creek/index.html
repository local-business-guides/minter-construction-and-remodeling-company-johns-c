What should a general contractor handle?

A general contractor organizes the work needed to complete a construction or remodeling project. Depending on the written agreement, that may include reviewing the existing space, setting a sequence for the work, coordinating subcontractors and deliveries, tracking progress, and communicating about decisions or changes.

The proposal should spell out the actual scope, not just name the room or project. Look for what is included in demolition, preparation, installation, cleanup, materials, subcontracted work, and any finish repairs. It should also say what is excluded and how the contractor will document extra work.

What does a local professional check before planning the work?

Before setting a firm scope, a contractor should look at the condition of the home and the details that can affect access and construction. In a kitchen or bathroom, for example, existing plumbing, wiring, ventilation, walls, and floor condition can change what needs to happen behind the visible finishes.

  • The project boundaries, including which rooms, surfaces, and fixtures are part of the work.
  • Access for workers, tools, material deliveries, and debris removal.
  • Existing conditions that may need repair or further investigation once finishes are opened.
  • The order of work and which trades need to finish before the next one can start.
  • Whether permits, inspections, association approval, or other local requirements need to be checked for the specific address and scope.

Requirements depend on the property, the work, and the authority responsible for that address. Ask who will confirm them and how required approvals will be handled before work begins.

What changes the cost of a general contractor project?

The price depends on the work being done, the materials selected, and the conditions found at the property. Moving plumbing or electrical connections, changing walls or openings, correcting hidden damage, and choosing custom finishes can add labor, materials, or coordination.

The amount of preparation matters too. A project may need protection for occupied areas, careful removal of existing finishes, repairs to surfaces, or more detailed cleanup. The proposal should identify allowances and owner-supplied items so you can see what is included and what could change.

Ask how unexpected conditions and requested changes are priced and approved. A clear written change process helps you understand the added work before it proceeds. Get comparable written scopes rather than relying on a single total or a verbal estimate.

What can affect the project schedule?

Timing depends on the scope, the sequence of trades, material availability, and whether approvals or inspections apply. Work can also take longer if demolition reveals a condition that was not visible during the initial visit, or if a selection changes after ordering.

Ask for a proposed start window, the main work stages, and how often you will hear about progress. A responsible schedule should account for dependencies, such as completing rough plumbing before closing a wall, rather than treating every task as if it can happen at once.

How should you check local rules and project permissions?

Do not assume that a project is permit-free because it is inside the home or described as a remodel. The work and property address determine which local rules, permit steps, inspections, or community approvals may apply.

Ask the contractor who will check the requirements, who will submit any needed paperwork, and whether inspections are included in the proposed plan. If the home is in an association or has property-specific restrictions, confirm those before ordering materials or setting a start date.

What hiring mistakes should you avoid?

  • Choosing from a headline price without checking the line-by-line scope and exclusions.
  • Starting work on a verbal agreement instead of a signed proposal that describes the project.
  • Assuming permits, inspections, cleanup, or protection of nearby rooms are included without asking.
  • Paying for additional work before its scope, cost, and schedule effect are documented.
  • Failing to ask who will be on site, who coordinates subcontractors, and who answers project questions.
  • Skipping checks on applicable insurance, qualifications, and local licensing requirements.

A quick conversation can uncover gaps before they become disputes. Make sure the final agreement reflects the decisions you discussed, including materials, responsibilities, payment milestones, and how changes are approved.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What work is included, and what is specifically excluded from this proposal?
  • Who will manage the project day to day and coordinate the subcontractors?
  • What permits, inspections, or other approvals may apply to this scope and address?
  • How do you handle hidden conditions discovered after demolition?
  • How will requested changes be priced, approved, and reflected in the schedule?
  • What materials or finish choices do I need to make, and by when?
  • How will the work area be protected, and what cleanup is included?
  • Can you provide proof of applicable insurance and qualifications for this work?
