What should a contractor handle?

A contractor should help turn your project idea into a defined scope of work, coordinate the people and materials needed, and keep you informed as the work moves along. The exact responsibilities depend on the agreement, so don't assume that scheduling, permits, inspections, cleanup, or ordering finishes are automatically included.

For a kitchen or bathroom remodel, the work may involve several trades and decisions about cabinets, fixtures, surfaces, and layout. Ask who is responsible for each part, who approves changes, and how you will hear about delays or issues. Put those answers in the proposal or contract.

What should you settle before requesting proposals?

You don't need every finish picked out before speaking with a contractor, but a shared understanding of the project makes proposals easier to compare. Describe what you want changed, what needs to stay, and any practical needs such as storage, accessibility, or keeping part of the home usable during construction.

  • List the rooms or areas involved and the main result you want.
  • Share photos, sketches, measurements, or inspiration images if you have them.
  • Identify decisions that are still open, such as materials, fixtures, or layout.
  • Mention any known water damage, previous repairs, or unusual site conditions.
  • Tell each provider about schedule needs and how much disruption your household can manage.

If the project is still taking shape, ask whether the contractor can help define the scope before preparing a firm proposal. Keep estimates tied to the same assumptions so you can see whether a difference comes from the work included, the materials, or something left out.

What changes the cost of a remodeling project?

There isn't a useful single price for contractors in Johns Creek because the amount depends on the work and the conditions at the property. A larger area, more involved layout changes, higher-cost finishes, custom work, and additional trades can all change the total. So can repairs uncovered after walls, floors, or fixtures are removed.

Check whether the proposal identifies labor, materials, allowances, exclusions, disposal, and any work that may be billed separately. An allowance is a placeholder for an item that hasn't been selected yet, so ask what happens if your final choice costs more or less. Get the process for approving added work in writing before construction begins.

If two proposals differ substantially, ask each contractor to explain the scope line by line. A lower total may leave out work or assume different materials, while a higher one may include tasks the other proposal doesn't cover.

What affects the project schedule?

Timing depends on the size and sequence of the work, the contractor's schedule, material availability, and how quickly selections and approvals are made. Permit review or inspections may also affect the sequence. Ask for an expected start window and a schedule that identifies the main stages, rather than relying only on a verbal completion date.

Find out how the contractor will handle delays, changes, and work that reveals an unexpected condition. Agree on how often you'll receive updates and who will contact you if a date shifts. If you need to keep using the kitchen, bathroom, or another part of the home, discuss that before the work plan is set.

Which local rules should you check?

Permit and inspection requirements depend on the property, the scope, and the authority that has jurisdiction over the address. Before work starts, confirm with the relevant local building department whether permits or inspections apply. If your home is in a community with an association, check whether it has separate review or work-hour requirements.

Ask the contractor who will prepare permit documents, submit applications, arrange inspections, and keep records. If the property is near a city boundary or you aren't sure which department handles it, verify the address directly rather than relying on a general assumption about the neighborhood.

What hiring mistakes can you avoid?

  • Choosing from a brief conversation alone instead of reviewing a written scope.
  • Comparing totals without checking that the same tasks and materials are included.
  • Leaving permit duties, cleanup, or disposal responsibilities unclear.
  • Making a change verbally without confirming its cost or schedule effect in writing.
  • Paying without understanding the payment schedule and what each payment covers.
  • Assuming a review rating or a polished proposal replaces questions about the actual project.

Before committing, read the agreement carefully and make sure the business name, scope, exclusions, payment terms, and change process are clear. If an important answer is missing, ask for it in writing. A little clarity at the start can prevent a disagreement once the work is underway.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • What work is included, and what is specifically excluded from this proposal?
  • Who will manage the project and be my main contact?
  • Which trades will be involved, and who coordinates their work?
  • Who checks permit and inspection requirements and handles those steps?
  • What materials or selections are allowances, and how are changes priced?
  • How will the schedule be shared, and how will you tell me about delays?
  • What is the payment schedule, and what does each payment cover?
  • Can I speak with customers whose projects were similar in scope?
