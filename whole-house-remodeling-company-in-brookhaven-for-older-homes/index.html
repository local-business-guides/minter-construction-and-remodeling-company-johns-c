What should a whole house remodel include?

A whole house remodel is a coordinated project across multiple areas, not simply a set of unrelated room updates. The plan may include a kitchen, bathrooms, flooring, interior finishes, layout changes, and needed repairs to building systems. The exact scope depends on the home's condition and the owner's priorities.

Before work starts, the written proposal should describe what is included, what is excluded, the finish selections or allowances, and how changes will be handled. It should also make clear who coordinates subcontractors and inspections, and how the work will be sequenced. That detail helps prevent an assumption about one room from turning into a dispute later.

What should a professional inspect in an older Brookhaven home?

A useful first visit looks beyond paint and cabinets. The contractor should ask what has been renovated before, look for visible signs of water entry or movement, and discuss the condition and capacity of the electrical, plumbing, heating, and cooling systems. Some issues cannot be confirmed until finishes are removed, so the proposal should explain how hidden conditions will be documented and priced.

The inspection should also cover the roof and exterior where they affect the project, access to work areas, and whether the proposed layout changes are practical. If older materials may contain lead or asbestos, don't disturb them casually. Ask how they will be assessed and have any needed testing or handling done by qualified professionals.

How can older-home conditions change the plan?

Older houses may have uneven floors, walls that are out of square, patched wiring, aging pipes, or previous work that is not visible at first. Those conditions do not automatically mean a project is unsafe or unworkable, but they can affect what needs repair and how new finishes fit. A careful contractor will separate confirmed work from items that may become necessary after demolition.

Ask for a plan for documenting discoveries, getting your approval, and recording any price or schedule adjustment before additional work proceeds. For layout changes, have the contractor explain which walls or systems need further evaluation before the design is treated as final. This is especially useful when the remodel combines rooms or relocates fixtures.

What affects the cost and timing?

Cost depends on the amount of work, the condition of existing materials and systems, the number of rooms, and whether the layout or plumbing and electrical locations will change. Finish choices matter too, as do custom details, access, disposal, and any repairs uncovered after demolition. No reliable total can be set without a defined scope and an assessment of the house.

Timing is shaped by the same conditions, along with design decisions, product availability, required inspections, and the order in which rooms can be worked on. Ask for a project schedule with major phases and decision deadlines, rather than relying on a single start-to-finish estimate. Confirm how delays, temporary loss of kitchen or bathroom access, and changes in scope will be communicated.

When reviewing proposals, compare the work and assumptions line by line, not just the bottom-line number. Check whether demolition, protection of areas that remain, cleanup, permits, finish materials, and repairs are included or treated separately.

What mistakes should homeowners avoid?

  • Starting demolition before the scope, selections, and approval process are clear.
  • Assuming a low bid includes the same materials, preparation, cleanup, or repair work as another proposal.
  • Treating a verbal promise about added work as a substitute for written approval and a documented change.
  • Setting a firm move-back date without allowing for inspections, deliveries, and concealed conditions.
  • Ignoring permits, neighborhood or HOA requirements, and rules that may apply to the property or planned work.

For a Brookhaven project, confirm which city or county requirements apply to the address and scope, and ask who will handle permit applications and inspections. If the property is subject to HOA rules or other restrictions, check those before ordering materials or scheduling construction.

Questions to Ask Checklist Before You Hire

  • Have you managed projects with a scope and age of home similar to mine?
  • What work is included in this proposal, and what is specifically excluded?
  • Who will coordinate the trades, inspections, and day-to-day schedule?
  • How do you document concealed conditions and get approval for added work?
  • Who is responsible for confirming permits and applicable local requirements?
  • What decisions or finish selections do you need from me, and by when?
  • How will you protect the parts of the house that are not being remodeled?
  • What is the expected sequence, and how will schedule changes be shared?

Listen for specific answers tied to your house, not just general assurances. A written proposal should reflect what you discussed and leave room to document decisions as the job moves forward.
