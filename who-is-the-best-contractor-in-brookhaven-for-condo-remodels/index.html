What should a condo remodel contractor handle?

A condo remodel can involve demolition, installation, finish work, and coordination with building management. The contractor's written scope should spell out which rooms and surfaces are included, what materials are being supplied, and what work is excluded. It should also explain who arranges inspections, manages trades, and handles debris and cleanup.

Condo work has another layer that a house remodel may not: shared spaces and building systems. Before work starts, the owner and contractor should understand rules for work hours, elevator use, deliveries, water shutoffs, protection of hallways, and access to the unit. The association may also require plans, insurance documents, or approval before construction begins.

What should a local professional check first?

A careful contractor starts by looking at the unit and asking about the building's requirements. For a kitchen or bath, that means checking the existing layout, visible plumbing and electrical connections, ventilation, and the condition of surfaces that will be covered. A site visit also helps identify whether the requested changes could affect shared systems or need design input.

  • Ask the association or property manager what approvals, insurance documents, and work-hour limits apply.
  • Confirm whether the project changes plumbing, electrical, ventilation, or walls, and who will confirm any related requirements.
  • Review how materials and waste will move through the building, including elevator reservations and protection for common areas.
  • Separate confirmed conditions from items that can only be assessed after demolition, such as concealed damage.

Permit and association requirements depend on the project and the building. Confirm current rules with the appropriate local office and the condo association rather than relying on assumptions or another unit owner's experience.

What changes the cost of a condo remodel?

The price depends on the scope, existing conditions, selected materials, and the coordination the building requires. Replacing finishes in the existing layout is different from moving plumbing, changing electrical locations, altering ventilation, or opening walls. Custom work and upgrades to fixtures or cabinetry can also change the labor and material needs.

Ask whether the proposal includes demolition, disposal, protection of common areas, installation, and final cleanup. Check how it treats permits or association paperwork, delivery coordination, material allowances, and work that cannot be confirmed until surfaces are opened. No contractor can responsibly give a firm total for unknown concealed conditions without explaining how those conditions will be priced and approved.

A useful estimate makes assumptions visible. If two bids differ, ask what is included in one and excluded from the other before deciding that either is the better value.

How long can a condo remodel take?

Timing depends on the amount of work, product availability, trade schedules, and how quickly the required building approvals are secured. A project that keeps the existing layout may have fewer coordination steps than one involving plumbing or electrical changes. The contractor should distinguish planning and approval time from the days when work is actively happening in the unit.

Ask for a schedule with key stages, including approvals, ordering, demolition, installation, inspections if needed, and final corrections. Also ask what could pause the work, how delays will be communicated, and what access the crew will need. A clear schedule is more useful than an unsupported promise of a specific finish date.

What mistakes should condo owners avoid?

  • Starting demolition before confirming association approval, building access, and any applicable permit requirements.
  • Choosing a bid by its total alone without comparing the written scope, materials, exclusions, and change process.
  • Assuming a contractor will arrange every building document or reserve the elevator without confirming that responsibility.
  • Leaving decisions about fixtures, finishes, and owner-supplied items until after work is underway.
  • Treating a verbal estimate or schedule as a substitute for written terms.

Before signing, get the project scope, payment schedule, expected timing, and process for approving extra work in writing. Keep association communications and contractor updates together so everyone is working from the same requirements.

Questions to ask before you hire

  • Have you completed condo projects with building rules similar to ours?
  • Who will coordinate with the association or property manager, and what documents do you need from me?
  • What work is included in this proposal, and what is specifically excluded?
  • Who will manage the project and be my regular contact?
  • How will you protect hallways, elevators, and other shared areas?
  • How do you handle concealed damage or other work that changes the original scope?
  • What schedule assumptions are you making, including approvals and material delivery?
  • Can I review the written contract, payment terms, and change-order process before I commit?
